# Who Decided Before You Pulled the Lever?

*Authority and Responsibility in the Trolley Problem*

The trolley problem places a decision in your hands. A runaway trolley will kill five people unless you divert it onto another track, where it will kill one. You know the outcomes. You have two available courses of action. You must decide whether to intervene.

Before you answer, the question has already determined who is exposed, what can be changed, what counts as intervention, and which consequences are certain. Your decision takes place inside this prior arrangement.

This construction makes the exercise possible. It also raises a question about what the exercise reveals: how much of the responsibility attributed to the respondent arises from the choice itself, and how much arises from the position the question has assigned?

The respondent receives a concentrated capacity to act. Moving the lever determines which group is struck. Yet the respondent has no stipulated control over the arrival of the trolley, the placement of the people, the absence of other interventions, or the time at which the choice becomes available.

Agency and control are therefore distributed unevenly. The respondent controls the final diversion; the question controls the conditions that make diversion the decisive act.

The author of a thought experiment does not thereby become morally responsible for fictional deaths. The relevant distinction is between authority over the construction of the decision-world and answerability within it. The exercise gives these to different positions. It then directs attention toward the person who must answer.

That direction matters. Once the available world is accepted, “What should you do?” can seem to contain the entire moral problem. The prior arrangement becomes background, while the respondent’s intervention or non-intervention becomes the object of judgment.

Within the stipulated emergency, this concentration can be useful. It lets us examine whether and why directing harm toward one person differs from allowing the trolley to continue toward five. Nothing about the construction alone invalidates that inquiry.

The difficulty arises when the final choice is made to represent responsibility for a larger arrangement.

Consider the difference between a bystander who encounters the lever moments before the collision and a designer determining how a transport system will operate. They occupy different positions in relation to the same possible deaths. The bystander encounters a constrained emergency. The designer may have earlier opportunities to alter operating conditions, introduce safeguards, or restrict where the system is used.

These opportunities must be established rather than assumed. Nevertheless, assigning both people the same final choice can conceal differences in what they could have changed and when.

An answer about what a bystander should do under fixed conditions cannot, by itself, determine which conditions a designer should authorise. The move from emergency judgment to design judgment requires another argument.

The same problem appears if the respondent is treated as a public decision-maker. Authority to set operating conditions involves questions about whose risks are accepted, whose interests are represented, and who may contest the arrangement. The lever offers no account of how that authority was obtained or bounded.

A respondent may therefore answer the numerical dilemma while leaving the authority question untouched. Alternatively, they may object to the assigned role and be treated as though they have failed to understand the dilemma.

The instruction “you have to choose” is particularly revealing. Inside the fictional emergency, it specifies the absence of another action. In a discussion of the problem, it can also operate as a rule governing which responses are admissible.

These functions should be distinguished. Accepting that a fictional bystander has only two options does not require treating those options as the only legitimate starting point for every inquiry into the case.

For example, “I would divert the trolley under these conditions” and “these conditions are inadequate for deciding how a transport system should be governed” can both be maintained. The second statement does not undo the first. It limits what may be inferred from it.

A refusal can also be less developed than this. Someone may reject the question because it is distressing, because they dislike either outcome, or because they believe additional options must exist. Such reactions do not automatically expose a defect in the exercise.

The objection becomes analytically useful when it identifies what the framing has settled and explains why that matters to the inquiry. If the issue concerns design responsibility, the exclusion of earlier design choices is relevant. If the issue concerns a tightly specified judgment about intervention, the same exclusion may be appropriate.

This provides a way to distinguish a substantive challenge from the general complaint that the case is unrealistic. Every abstraction leaves things out. The question is whether an exclusion removes something that could change the conclusion the exercise is being used to support.

In an automated-transport discussion, this distinction changes the object of analysis. Asking which person a machine should sacrifice concentrates attention on a final allocation of harm. Asking how people and institutions authorise the conditions under which the machine operates introduces decisions about exposure, uncertainty, intervention, and revision.

The final emergency remains relevant. Its place within responsibility changes.

We would need to examine which choices were available before the emergency, who controlled them, and what information could have altered them. We would also need to distinguish responsibility for creating an exposure from responsibility for responding to it. The two can overlap, but neither can be inferred solely from possession of the last available control.

There is a further danger in moving earlier: imagining a designer or regulator who commands the whole system. That would reproduce the concentrated decision position at another point. A wider analysis must establish actual capacities, dependencies, and constraints. Earlier does not automatically mean more adequate.

The strength of the trolley problem is the clarity of its constructed choice. Its limit is that this clarity can make the construction disappear from attention. We see a respondent deciding between deaths and can overlook how the exercise has made that respondent the focal bearer of responsibility.

This is a form of command-position universalisation: a stipulated standpoint with final decisional power becomes a general standpoint from which an ethical problem is approached. What a particular person could know, authorise, or change is replaced by what “you” must decide inside the exercise.

The resulting analysis may be internally careful while remaining incomplete for the larger question. It can produce a sophisticated account of choosing within the arrangement without establishing whether that arrangement is an adequate representation of the responsibility being studied.

The case therefore invites two connected inquiries. One concerns what the respondent should do with the lever. The other concerns what has already been decided in order to make the lever the place where responsibility appears.

A satisfactory use of the trolley problem should make the relationship between those inquiries explicit. Its conclusions should extend as far as its construction can support, and further claims should recover the decisions, positions, and capacities that the exercise set aside.

*This analysis draws on the Protocol of Right-Leveling (PRL), using the simplified lever version of the trolley problem as its object of inquiry.*
