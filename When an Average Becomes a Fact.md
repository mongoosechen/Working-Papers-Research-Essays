# When an Average Becomes a Fact

*The Changing Authority of a Mathematical Object*

An annual income total can tell us how much a person received over a year. Dividing it by 52 produces a weekly average. Neither operation establishes how much the person received in any particular week. Yet the calculated average can enter a decision process as if that further question had already been answered.

The important transition occurs when the result acquires a new role. A number produced by aggregation becomes evidence about circumstances that aggregation no longer preserves. Once decisions depend on it, the distinction between these roles becomes consequential: a person may have to contest an asserted fact that was never independently established.

Consider a simplified example. Two people each receive $26,000 over 52 weeks. The first receives $500 every week. The second receives $1,000 per week for 26 weeks and nothing for the remaining 26. Both have a weekly average of $500.

Their equality under the calculation coexists with a difference in their circumstances. If a hypothetical decision concerns whether income exceeded $600 during a particular week, the first person never crossed the threshold; the second crossed it in 26 weeks. The annual total and its weekly average are insufficient to distinguish them.

The calculation has done exactly what it was asked to do. It has produced a relationship between a total and a period. The difficulty begins when the result is asked to stand for a distribution of events within that period.

There are now two claims attached to the same value. “The weekly average is $500” follows from the stated total and period. “The person received $500 in this week” requires further evidence or an additional assumption. Moving between them changes the claim while leaving the visible number unchanged.

This continuity can make the transition difficult to notice. A familiar value appears in successive records, and each appearance can seem to confirm that the same information is being carried forward. What persists, however, may be the numerical expression rather than the justification for its current use.

Suppose an administrative process enters the average into a weekly assessment field. That field participates in a rule, and the rule produces a decision. The average now has an operational role: it helps determine what the institution does.

The record’s category matters as much as its numerical content. A value labelled “estimated weekly average” invites different inferences from one labelled “weekly income.” Removing the qualification can change how the object is understood without changing its digits. A later user may have access to the populated field but little reason, time, or permission to recover how it was populated.

The resulting authority is jointly produced. The calculation gives the value formal support within a limited domain. The institution selects the field, accepts the value as relevant evidence, connects it to a rule, and acts on the outcome. Each contribution enables the next.

This explains why the problem cannot be located entirely inside the arithmetic. Repeating the division will reproduce the same correct answer. Nor is it adequately explained by saying that an institution simply chose to ignore a fact. The arrangement can organise work so that the limited result is what staff encounter as the fact.

A useful way to examine the transition is to follow what would have to be established at each point. Producing an average requires the total, the period, and the operation. Describing a particular week requires information about that week or a defensible basis for estimation. Authorising a consequential decision requires a further account of whether that evidence is adequate for the decision and what uncertainty remains.

These requirements do not disappear when one result is reused. They can, however, become less visible.

Imagine the affected person challenges the decision by saying that their income varied. An official checks the annual total and confirms that the division was performed correctly. Both participants may be discussing the same number while disputing different propositions.

The official has verified the production of the average. The person is challenging its use as a description of particular weeks. If verification of the first proposition is accepted as an answer to the second, the review repeats the original substitution.

The person may then be asked to supply detailed records to displace the average. The practical burden becomes asymmetric. Producing the default claim required an aggregate and a calculation; overturning its application may require reconstructing many individual events. An informational shortcut for the institution becomes an evidential workload for the person.

That asymmetry does not establish that every use of an estimate is improper. Estimation may be necessary when detailed information is unavailable. It does establish a question about the status of the estimate: does the process continue treating it as provisional, or does its use make it increasingly difficult to dislodge?

The distinction is visible in what happens when contrary information arrives. A revisable estimate can be replaced, and the dependent decision can be reconsidered. An estimate treated as an established fact may instead become the standard against which contrary information is judged suspicious or insufficient.

The number’s history also matters. If several later records derive from the same initial average, their agreement is not independent confirmation. Repetition can increase apparent solidity without adding evidence. A challenge that examines only the final record may therefore miss the common source of what appears to be corroboration.

The mathematical object has acquired several layers of support: correctness of calculation, acceptance into a record, use by a procedure, and reliance by subsequent decisions. These layers can reinforce one another while answering different questions. Their combined authority can exceed the support available for the claim about the person’s actual circumstances.

Changing this arrangement requires locating the unsupported transition. Correcting the annual total would not resolve a problem caused by treating the average as a weekly observation. Adding an uncertainty label would have limited effect if the decision rule continued using the value as conclusive. Revising the decision would remain incomplete if dependent records retained the earlier claim.

What must remain recoverable is the relationship among the object, its warrant, and its use. What was calculated? What information did the operation preserve? What did its new role require? Which additional assumption supplied the difference? Who could challenge that assumption, and what would change if the challenge succeeded?

The case shows how a mathematical object can retain its formal identity while undergoing a substantial change in epistemic and institutional status. Its authority develops through the relationships that let it travel, settle questions, and initiate action.

An average becomes an asserted fact about a particular week at the point where the distinction between calculation and observation ceases to govern its use. Understanding that point allows a more precise challenge: the question is whether this result establishes this claim for this decision.

*This analysis draws on Four Epistemic Orientations (FEO). The numerical example and administrative sequence are hypothetical and serve to isolate the transformations under examination.*
